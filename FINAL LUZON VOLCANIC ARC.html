🌋 What is the Luzon Volcanic Arc?

A chain of volcanoes running roughly north–south across the Luzon Strait — from Taiwan down through the Batanes and Babuyan islands onto Luzon itself. It is not only the volcanoes on Luzon Island, but the whole island arc between Taiwan and the Philippines.

How it formed

The arc was created by eastward subduction along the Manila Trench: the Eurasian Plate (Sundaland Block) sinks beneath the Philippine Mobile Belt. Subduction began in the late Oligocene to early Miocene (around 16 million years ago in the north). The trench is about 1,200 km long and subducts at roughly 1–2 cm per year. Because of this, the arc is also called the Manila Trench volcanic system. Volcanoes are generally younger toward the south than near Taiwan.

Tectonic plates colliding to form the arc

The Philippine Volcanic Island Arc System is created by a complex collision between two primary tectonic plates squeezing a microplate area known as the Philippine Mobile Belt:

  • Eurasian Plate (Sunda Plate Block) — Pushes and subducts from the west along trenches like the Manila, Negros, Sulu, and Cotabato Trenches.
  • Philippine Sea Plate — Pushes and subducts from the east beneath the archipelago along the Philippine Trench and East Luzon Trough.

This opposing subduction forces oceanic crust deep underground where it melts into magma that feeds the volcanoes.

How long has this volcanic chain been active?

  • Inception of early arcs: The oldest volcanic arc rocks that built the foundation of the Philippine archipelago began forming about 40 to 50 million years ago (during the Eocene epoch).
  • Modern active trenches & volcanic chains: The current active subduction system and modern volcanic chains (like Mayon, Taal, Pinatubo, and Apo) developed much later, taking shape over the past 2 to 15 million years (from the Miocene epoch through the Quaternary period), and remain fully active today.

Summary: While the ancient arc systems started forming over 45 million years ago, the current active subduction trenches and modern volcanic chains have been actively shaping the archipelago for the last few million years and continue to fuel frequent volcanic eruptions and seismic activity today.

Volcanicity of the Luzon Volcanic Arc

The volcanoes of the arc are typical of a subduction-related island arc. Most are andesitic stratovolcanoes, but rock compositions range from basalt to rhyolite.

  • Magma series: Mainly calc-alkaline (the classic arc type). Older centers are often low-K tholeiitic; younger ones can be medium- to high-K calc-alkaline.
  • Chemical signature: Strong subduction fingerprints — enrichment in large-ion lithophile elements, low TiO2, and clear negative Nb–Ta–Ti anomalies.
  • Eruption styles: Range from explosive (Plinian, Vulcanian, phreatomagmatic) to effusive lava flows. Common hazards include pyroclastic flows, ash falls, and lahars (especially after heavy rain).
  • Landforms: Mostly steep stratovolcanoes (Mayon, Pinatubo, Babuyan Claro). Taal is a large caldera with an active island cone; Didicas is a compound volcano that grew from the sea.
  • Geochemical domains: Five segments with distinct chemistry — Mindoro, Bataan, Northern Luzon, Babuyan, and Taiwan — reflecting differences in slab input, sediment thickness, and crustal contamination along the arc.

Activity remains high today. Pinatubo’s 1991 eruption was one of the largest of the 20th century; Taal and Mayon continue to produce frequent eruptions that are closely monitored by PHIVOLCS.

Geographic extent

About 1,200 km from the Coastal Range of Taiwan south to southern Mindoro / the Bataan area of Luzon.

  • Northern end (Taiwan segment): Eastern Coastal Range, Green Island, Orchid Island, Kaotai Rock
  • Middle (Bashi Channel / Luzon Strait): Y’ami (Mavudis), Mabudis, Siayan, Itbayat, Diogo, Batan, Ibuhos, Sabtang
  • Southern end (Babuyan to Luzon): Babuyan Islands, Didicas, Camiguin de Babuyanes, then main Luzon — including Mt. Cagua, the Zambales–Bataan arc (Pinatubo, Natib, Mariveles), Taal, and related centers toward Mindoro

Geochemistry divides the arc into five domains: Mindoro, Bataan, Northern Luzon, Babuyan, and Taiwan. Mindanao has separate arcs (e.g. Central Mindanao, Sangihe); the Luzon Volcanic Arc is the Manila Trench system affecting western Luzon.

The “Double Island Arc” (northern segment)

In the northernmost part (Luzon Strait / Babuyan–Taiwan area), the arc splits into two parallel chains instead of one:

Western Arc (Front / Volcanic) Camiguin Norte, Babuyan Claro, Calayan, Dalupiri, and related islands — built by active volcanoes from magma above the subducting plate.
Eastern Arc (Back / Non-volcanic) Batanes and the Hengchun Ridge of southern Taiwan — mostly uplifted older rocks and coral limestone, pushed up by plate collision and compression.

One chain is volcanic (melted rock); the other is uplifted land (squeezed upward) — running side by side. That is why it is called a “double” island arc.

What happens to the South China Sea slab under the Manila Trench?

  1. Subduction begins — The South China Sea plate (part of the Eurasian Plate) moves east and sinks beneath the Philippine archipelago along the Manila Trench.
  2. Heating — At roughly 100–150 km depth, the slab becomes extremely hot.
  3. Water released — Minerals in the oceanic crust release trapped water, which lowers the melting point of the mantle above.
  4. Magma forms — Mantle rock melts into magma.
  5. Magma rises — Less dense than solid rock, it pushes upward through the crust.
  6. Volcanoes form — Magma reaches the surface and feeds the Luzon Volcanic Arc (Pinatubo, Taal, Mayon, Babuyan volcanoes, and others).
  7. Deep recycling — The slab continues sinking into the deep mantle.

In short: sinks → heats → releases water → creates magma → feeds volcanoes → returns to the deep mantle.

Famous volcanoes on the arc

  • Mayon — Near-perfect symmetrical cone; most active in the Philippines.
  • Taal — Volcano island inside a lake; highly active and closely monitored.
  • Pinatubo — Catastrophic 1991 eruption after centuries of dormancy.
  • Babuyan group — Smith, Babuyan Claro, Didicas (emerged above sea level in the 1950s), and others.

Pinatubo 1991: One of the largest eruptions of the 20th century; injected sulfur dioxide into the stratosphere and cooled global climate by about 0.5 °C; joint PHIVOLCS–USGS forecasting enabled mass evacuations that saved tens of thousands of lives; subsequent lahars reshaped the landscape and influenced hazard science worldwide.

Hazard exposure is assessed volcano by volcano (PHIVOLCS maps). Population at risk can reach into the millions depending on which zones and year are counted — there is no single official total for the entire arc.
